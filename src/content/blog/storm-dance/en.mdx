## Why

This is the famous scene from the GENER8ION and Yung Lean video for "STORM" (April 2026, directed by Romain Gavras): a boarding school in Leeds in 2034, a hundred boys in uniform lined up on the steps of a brick schoolhouse for a class photo, and only the one in the white shirt stands still while the rest fold and rear back in waves. It picked up four VMA nominations this year, and then the "me doing STORM" videos took over. Most were made with video-to-video motion transfer tools like Higgsfield, and the director [complained publicly](https://stereogum.com/2515160/romain-gavras-addresses-slop-copies-of-gener8ion-yung-lean-storm-video/news) about the AI copies.

I wanted one too, but this blog has three rules: client-side only, zero server cost, no external APIs. A video diffusion model breaks all three. So I took numbers only from the video and no pixels at all: the lead's joint coordinates, a motion field of the crowd, and the speed at which the camera pulls back. Everything else is drawn again in three.js.

## 1. The lead, from joints

I used MediaPipe's Pose Landmarker. Give it one image and it returns 33 joints, including **3D coordinates in metres** with the origin between the hips, which is what makes a turn come out as a turn.

The problem was a hundred people in every frame. "The biggest person nearest the centre" hopped to a neighbour every few frames. In the end I found him by his shirt. Yung Lean is the only one without a blazer in this scene, so convert the frame to HSV, take the largest bright, unsaturated blob, and that's him. Crop a body-sized window around it, scale it up, run the pose model on that, and keep only a pose whose torso sits on the blob. It holds even in wide shots where he's five percent of the frame. The nine seconds where he smokes, turns and walks into the crowd show only his back, so I cut them.

Pinning the coordinates straight onto the doll looked wrong. That was the first version, and someone who saw it asked whether I'd made the dance bad on purpose. I hadn't. MediaPipe's 3D coordinates flatten the torso when someone bends, stretch the arms, and tilt the whole person backwards when the camera looks down. So instead of using the coordinates as they are, I take only the lean. Measure the forward and sideways angle of the hips-to-shoulders direction, tilt a fixed-length spine by that much, and put the shoulders back on top of it. The arms take their tracked bone directions, capped in length, and hang from the new shoulders; the head is re-hung at its tracked position relative to the shoulders. The hand going to the mouth and the fiddling with the collar survive, and the folding torso doesn't. The legs never appear in the frame, so they just stand.

## 2. The crowd, from flow

At first I tried to extract the crowd one person at a time too. I wrote the script: tile the frame, find several people per tile, merge the overlaps, use each boy's spot as his identity and stitch the frames together. The result was zero to two people per frame. While they stand still it finds a dozen; the moment the dance starts they all vanish. Bodies folded at ninety degrees, navy blazer on navy blazer, heads down at the chest of the boy beside them: the pose model does not see people there.

So I stopped counting people and measured motion. Optical flow (Farneback) between consecutive frames gives, per pixel, where it moved and by how much. Divide the frame into a 12×7 grid, average each cell, and use the lead's shirt height as the unit. The camera is slowly pulling back, which puts an outward drift across the whole frame; that gets removed by fitting one zoom-plus-translation to every pixel by least squares and subtracting it. What's left is the people.

More useful than the speed itself is how far a patch has been pushed lately. Accumulate each cell's velocity with a leak (0.7 s time constant) and you get a displacement that piles up downwards during a bow and comes back on the way up. Alongside it, the speed's magnitude minus the magnitude of the averaged speed is kept as a "shaking" value, the part of the motion that isn't going anywhere. At 12 frames per second, 84 cells and three integers per cell, 25 seconds is 240KB, or 56KB compressed.

In the 3D scene each figure's spot is expressed relative to the lead, "so many shirts to the right and so many below", and every frame that spot is turned into a grid cell using where the shirt was and how big it was at that moment. As the camera pulls back and the shirt shrinks, the same figure moves to a nearer cell. If the cell's displacement points down, the figure bows; up, it rears back and throws its head; sideways, it leans; if the shaking is high, it balls its fists at its chest and trembles. Each figure gets a slightly different gain and delay so the four or five in one cell don't move like clones.

What comes out is a map of which way the crowd at each spot was pushed at each moment, and since that is what the crowd choreography in this scene consists of, the wave crossing from left to right, the front rows folding first and the back rows following, all land at the same time as the video.

One thing came out of it as a bonus. I had assumed 4:30 to 4:55 was several cuts, but the frame differences say there are none. It's one shot that pulls slowly from a medium to a wide.

## 3. The set and the camera

The bricks are a repeating texture of staggered rectangles drawn on a canvas; the arched doorway is a Shape extruded backwards into a dark recess; the maroon canopy over it is one bent band; the windows are canvases with white lattices; and the "Class of 2034" banners on either side are canvases with rotated text. The steps have eight tiers with seventeen boys on each. The lead is in the middle of the fifth row.

A hundred and thirty-six figures as separate meshes would be over three thousand draw calls. Instead the cylinders, spheres, boxes, ties and face ovals are each one InstancedMesh, with a transform and a colour per figure. The whole crowd is five draw calls, and skin and hair colours are instance colours. Every frame computes 17 joints per figure and fits 24 parts between them, and it runs at 120fps on a laptop.

The camera is my favourite part. Since the shirt's height as a fraction of the frame was measured for every frame, a long lens (16 degrees) just needs the distance at which the shirt appears at that fraction: distance = shirt height ÷ (2 × tan(fov/2) × fraction). It pulls back exactly as the video does. Where the shirt sat in the frame was measured too, so the lead lands in the same place on screen. The three-second close-up at the start uses a wide lens (36 degrees) 85 centimetres in front of the face, with the camera between the heads of the two boys in the row ahead.

## 4. The face

Choose a photo and a canvas with an oval appears. Drag to fit your face, scroll or pinch to resize. On confirm, the oval region is copied to a new canvas and multiplied, using the `destination-in` composite mode, by a radial gradient that fades at the edge. That canvas becomes a three.js texture on the front of the head. The photo comes in through `<input type="file">` and is used only as an `Image` object. There is no path to a server.

The classmates share one texture of eyes, brows and a mouth on a white ground, multiplied by each figure's skin colour as an instance colour, so they look different. "My face on everyone" swaps that texture for your photo.

## Why the real version can't run in a browser

The "me doing STORM" videos are video-to-video motion transfer. The motion of the original is the condition, and the model generates new pixels of the person in the photo performing it. That's a video diffusion model: billions of parameters, tens of gigabytes of memory, and somewhere between tens of seconds and minutes on a server GPU for a clip a few seconds long. Even with WebGPU, the weights won't fit in a page. Send it to a server and every run costs money, and at that point it's a service, not a useless toy.

This post sits at the opposite end. Understanding the video (where the joints are, which patch moved where) is done by a model of a few megabytes or by classic image processing, and the drawing is done by three.js at sixty frames a second. The only things on the page are 113KB of joints and 56KB of flow.

## What it doesn't do

It is not the real motion of each boy in the crowd. People in the same cell move alike. The lead's legs are invented because they never appear in the video, and when his head looks the wrong way for a moment that's tracking error. The dolls are still dolls. But the hundred and thirty-five of them heaving in the same places, at the same times, in the same directions, and the camera pulling back in front of them, all come from the video, and from a distance it is that scene.
