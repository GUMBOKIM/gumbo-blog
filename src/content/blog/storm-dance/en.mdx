Second entry in the [useless things series](/en/posts/useless-ui/). Put in a photo of your face and it goes on the boy in the white shirt, while a hundred and thirty-five others in blazers do the video's routine around you. It's the close-up at 4:18 and the class-photo scene from 4:30 to 4:55.

<StormDoll locale="en" />

- **Choose a photo** of your own face and fit it inside the oval, and the lead wears it. The photo stays in your browser and is never sent anywhere.
- **My face on everyone** gives all hundred and thirty-six the same face.
- The camera pulls back from the same spot at the same speed as the video. Drag to take it over, and **Back to the video camera** returns it.
- Turn off **Classmates** to stand there alone.

## Why

This is the famous scene from the GENER8ION and Yung Lean video for "STORM" (April 2026, directed by Romain Gavras): a boarding school in Leeds in 2034, a hundred boys in uniform lined up on the steps of a brick schoolhouse for a class photo, and only the one in the white shirt stands still while the rest fold and rear back on the beat. It picked up four VMA nominations this year, and then the "me doing STORM" videos took over. Most were made with video-to-video motion transfer tools like Higgsfield, and the director [complained publicly](https://stereogum.com/2515160/romain-gavras-addresses-slop-copies-of-gener8ion-yung-lean-storm-video/news) about the AI copies.

I wanted one too, but this blog has three rules: client-side only, zero server cost, no external APIs. A video diffusion model breaks all three. So I took numbers only from the video and no pixels at all: the lead's joint coordinates, the speed at which the camera pulls back, and the beat of the crowd's routine. Everything else is drawn again in three.js.

## 1. The lead, from joints

I used MediaPipe's Pose Landmarker. Give it one image and it returns 33 joints, including 3D coordinates in metres with the origin between the hips.

The problem was a hundred people in every frame. "The biggest person nearest the centre" hopped to a neighbour every few frames. In the end I found him by his shirt. Yung Lean is the only one without a blazer in this scene, so convert the frame to HSV, take the largest bright, unsaturated blob, and that's him. Crop a body-sized window around it, scale it up, run the pose model on that, and keep only a pose whose torso sits on the blob. The nine seconds where he smokes, turns and walks into the crowd show only his back, so I cut them.

Pinning the coordinates straight onto the doll looked wrong. That was the first version, and someone who saw it asked whether I'd made the dance bad on purpose. I hadn't. MediaPipe's 3D coordinates flatten the torso when someone bends, stretch the arms, and tilt the whole person backwards when the camera looks down. So instead of using the coordinates as they are, I take only the lean. Measure the forward and sideways angle of the hips-to-shoulders direction, tilt a fixed-length spine by that much, and put the shoulders back on top of it. The arms take their tracked bone directions, capped in length, and hang from the new shoulders; the head is re-hung at its tracked position relative to the shoulders. The hand going to the mouth and the fiddling with the collar survive, and the folding torso doesn't. The legs never appear in the frame, so they just stand.

## 2. The crowd, from the beat

At first I tried to extract the crowd one person at a time too. I wrote the script: tile the frame, find several people per tile, merge the overlaps, use each boy's spot as his identity and stitch the frames together. The result was zero to two people per frame. While they stand still it finds a dozen; the moment the dance starts they all vanish. Bodies folded at ninety degrees, navy blazer on navy blazer, heads down at the chest of the boy beside them: the pose model does not see people there.

Next I tried optical flow on a grid: measure which way each cell of the frame had been pushed, and have each figure bow and rear by the cell it stood in. That was worse. With every cell following its own value, a hundred and thirty-five figures fluttered like grass in the wind, each a little differently. The video is the opposite of that. It's a drilled, unison routine.

So I dropped the cells and plotted the mean vertical flow of the whole frame at 25 frames per second. Out came one clean waveform.

- At 4:38.9 everyone folds forward at once (head down). 0.7 seconds later they rear back (face to the sky). A brief settle, then fold again. Period 1.72 seconds, four times.
- From 4:44.7 a side-to-side sway is added on top, still reared back: left, right, left, right. Period 0.875 seconds, exactly half the fold-and-rear period, which carries on, shallower, every 1.75 seconds.
- From 4:53 everyone is upright, fists at the chest, trembling, with small nods.
- The lead stands still throughout.

That the whole-frame mean comes out as a clean waveform is itself the proof that everyone does the same thing on the same beat; if they moved independently the average would smear towards zero. So the crowd's motion isn't data the figures follow. It is written as keyframes: the times of the four fold bottoms and the four rear tops, the angles in between, the head angle and where the arms are, then the sway as a cosine over the next stretch, then the upright trembling. It's a hundred-line file. All hundred and thirty-five figures dance that one routine, each row 0.035 seconds behind the one in front, each person within 0.05 seconds of the beat, with the amplitude varied by eight percent. That much is enough for a wave to roll back through the rows and for it to look like people.

When they fold, the arms hang from the shoulders straight down towards the ground; when they rear, the fists come up to the chest and turn with the torso. Both are what the video shows. The flow script was used to read the routine and then keeps only one number for the camera.

## 3. The set and the camera

The bricks are a repeating texture of staggered rectangles drawn on a canvas; the arched doorway is a Shape extruded backwards into a dark recess; the maroon canopy over it is one bent band; the windows are canvases with white lattices; and the "Class of 2034" banners on either side are canvases with rotated text. The steps have eight tiers with seventeen boys on each. The lead is in the middle of the fifth row.

A hundred and thirty-six figures as separate meshes would be over three thousand draw calls. Instead the cylinders, spheres, boxes, ties and face ovals are each one InstancedMesh, with a transform and a colour per figure. The whole crowd is five draw calls, and skin and hair colours are instance colours. Every frame computes 17 joints per figure and fits 24 parts between them, and it runs at 120fps on a laptop.

The camera is my favourite part. Since the white shirt's height as a fraction of the frame, and where it sits, were measured for every frame, a long lens (16 degrees) just needs the distance at which the shirt appears at that fraction: distance = shirt height ÷ (2 × tan(fov/2) × fraction). It pulls back exactly as the video does and the lead lands in the same place on screen. One thing came out of this as a bonus: I had assumed 4:30 to 4:55 was several cuts, but the frame differences say there are none. It's one shot that pulls slowly from a medium to a wide. The three-second close-up at the start uses a wide lens (36 degrees) 85 centimetres in front of the face, with the camera between the heads of the two boys in the row ahead.

## 4. The face

Choose a photo and a canvas with an oval appears. Drag to fit your face, scroll or pinch to resize. On confirm, the oval region is copied to a new canvas and multiplied, using the `destination-in` composite mode, by a radial gradient that fades at the edge. That canvas becomes a three.js texture on the front of the head. The photo comes in through `<input type="file">` and is used only as an `Image` object. There is no path to a server.

The classmates share one texture of eyes, brows and a mouth on a white ground, multiplied by each figure's skin colour as an instance colour, so they look different. "My face on everyone" swaps that texture for your photo.

## Why the real version can't run in a browser

The "me doing STORM" videos are video-to-video motion transfer. The motion of the original is the condition, and the model generates new pixels of the person in the photo performing it. That's a video diffusion model: billions of parameters, tens of gigabytes of memory, and somewhere between tens of seconds and minutes on a server GPU for a clip a few seconds long. Even with WebGPU, the weights won't fit in a page. Send it to a server and every run costs money, and at that point it's a service, not a useless toy.

This post sits at the opposite end. Understanding the video (where the joints are, when the crowd moves which way) is done by a model of a few megabytes or by classic image processing, and the drawing is done by three.js at sixty frames a second. The only things on the page are 113KB of joints, 7KB of camera, and a hundred lines of choreography.

## What it doesn't do

It is not the real motion of each boy in the crowd. Everyone dances the same routine slightly out of step, and if someone in the video did his own thing, he isn't here. The lead's legs are invented because they never appear in the video, and when his head looks the wrong way for a moment that's tracking error. The dolls are still dolls. But the beat of the folding, rearing and swaying, and the speed of the camera pulling back, were measured from the video, and from a distance it is that scene.

The doll and set code and the choreography keyframes are in the [repository](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/src/components/storm), and the scripts that find the shirt, extract the joints and measure the flow are in [scripts/storm-pose](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/scripts/storm-pose).
