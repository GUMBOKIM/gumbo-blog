Second entry in the [useless things series](/en/posts/useless-ui/). This time, put in a photo of your face and a paper doll in a school uniform dances the STORM choreography.

<StormDoll locale="en" />

- **Choose a photo** of your own face and fit it inside the oval, and the doll dances with it. The photo stays in your browser and is never sent anywhere.
- Turn off **Classmates** to dance alone. Leave it on and fourteen copies of the same face follow half a beat behind.
- **Show skeleton** puts the raw joint coordinates from the video in the top right. That's all the doll has to go on.

## Why make it bad on purpose

You've probably seen the GENER8ION and Yung Lean video for "STORM" (April 2026, directed by Romain Gavras): a boarding school in Leeds in 2034, a hundred boys in uniform lined up for a class photo, and that choreography. It picked up four VMA nominations this year, and then the "me doing STORM" videos took over. Most were made with video-to-video motion transfer tools like Higgsfield, and the director [complained publicly](https://stereogum.com/2515160/romain-gavras-addresses-slop-copies-of-gener8ion-yung-lean-storm-video/news) about the AI copies.

I wanted one too, but this blog has three rules: client-side only, zero server cost, no external APIs. A video diffusion model breaks all three. So I went the other way. Take only the joint coordinates from the video, cut the face out of a photo as an oval, and draw the body on a canvas like a paper doll. Give up on looking real from the start and you get something that runs in the browser immediately. And the badness is pretty funny.

## How it's built

### 1. Pulling the joints out of the video

I used MediaPipe's Pose Landmarker. Give it one image and it returns the positions of 33 joints: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles and so on. Coordinates are 0 to 1 fractions of the frame's width and height, and each joint carries its own visibility score. Legs cut off by the frame still get estimated coordinates, but with low visibility.

I saved the music video locally and ran the frames through Python. My first pass simply took "the biggest person nearest the centre", but with a hundred people moving at once the tracker kept hopping to a neighbour. Only after adding a preference for whoever's shoulders were closest to the previous frame's pick did it stick to Yung Lean. Shot changes still break it, so I kept one 16-second stretch where the central figure is large.

Then cleanup. Frames where the shoulders sit below the hips or the person drifts to the edge of the frame mean the tracker grabbed someone else, so those are dropped. Gaps are interpolated from their neighbours and a three-frame average takes the jitter out. Keep the 15 of the 33 joints the doll needs, store coordinates as integers from 0 to 1000, and 16 seconds at 15 frames per second comes to a 43KB JSON file. Those numbers are the only thing on the page. Not a single frame of video is included.

### 2. Angles only, never raw positions

Connect the raw coordinates and you get the stick figure under "Show skeleton". It moves when the camera moves, changes size when the shot changes, and sends hidden legs off somewhere strange.

The paper doll works differently. Arm length, leg length and torso length are fixed, and the video contributes only the angle of each bone: shoulder to elbow, elbow to wrist, hip to shoulder, and so on. Put the origin at the hip centre and attach bones one by one at those angles and the doll appears.

```ts
const torsoAngle = angle(hipCenter, shoulderCenter);
const neckBase = polar(origin, torsoAngle, RIG.torso);
const shoulderL = polar(neckBase, shoulderAngle, RIG.shoulderHalf);
const elbowL = polar(shoulderL, angle(shoulderL_src, elbowL_src), RIG.upperArm);
const wristL = polar(elbowL, angle(elbowL_src, wristL_src), RIG.foreArm);
```

That's the whole of 2D rigging. Whether the camera zooms or the dancer is anywhere in the frame, the angles stay the same, so the doll dances in place. Only the sideways movement of the hips in the video is taken separately, to add a little sway.

Hidden joints remember their angle. The source segment is mostly upper body, so knee and ankle visibility drops often, and a leg that snaps every time that happens looks awful. The angle updates only when both joints are clearly visible; otherwise the last one holds. The doll's legs are honestly about half guesswork.

Head roll is the angle between the two ears, and how far the head is turned comes from how far the nose sits from the midpoint of the ears. Squeeze the face oval by that much and push it toward the nose, and it passes for looking sideways.

### 3. The face is cut out as an oval

The original plan was to run MediaPipe's face segmentation in the browser and trace the hairline automatically. But MediaPipe for the browser needs a wasm runtime and a model file from a CDN, several megabytes together. I could vendor them into the repository, but a glued-on paper doll didn't seem to need that.

So a human does the cutting. Choose a photo and a canvas with an oval appears. Drag to fit your face, scroll or pinch to resize. On confirm, the oval region is copied to a new canvas and multiplied, using the `destination-in` composite mode, by a radial gradient that fades at the edge. A white border goes around it for the paper look.

The photo comes in through `<input type="file">` and is used only as an `Image` object. There is no path to a server.

### 4. Classmates

The fun of the original is a hundred people doing the same thing. So fourteen more stand behind. They play the same motion data a few frames late, smaller, darker, and in blazers, each with their own angle memory. They all have the same face. This is the worst part and the funniest.

## Why the real version can't run in a browser

The "me doing STORM" videos are video-to-video motion transfer. The motion of the original is the condition, and the model generates new pixels of the person in the photo performing it. That's a video diffusion model: billions of parameters, tens of gigabytes of memory, and somewhere between tens of seconds and minutes on a server GPU for a clip a few seconds long. Even with WebGPU, the weights won't fit in a page, and even if they did you'd be staring at a progress bar instead of touching anything. Send it to a server and every run costs money, and at that point it's a service, not a useless toy.

Pose estimation sits at the opposite end. The model is a few megabytes and its whole job is answering "where are the joints" with numbers, so it runs in real time on a phone. The doll in this post is that difference made visible. Understanding motion is cheap and rendering it is expensive. Give up on rendering and use a paper doll, and the rest is free.

## Wrapping up

My favourite thing about the result is switching on "Show skeleton". The stick figure in the corner and the paper doll in the middle are visibly looking at the same numbers. Those numbers are the only thing taken from the video. Everything else is drawn.

The doll's code is in the [repository](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/src/components/storm), and the Python scripts that extracted and cleaned the joints are in [scripts/storm-pose](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/scripts/storm-pose). They're the MediaPipe Pose Landmarker example with one extra condition for following the person in the middle.
