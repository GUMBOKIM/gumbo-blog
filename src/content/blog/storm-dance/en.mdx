- Turn off **Classmates** to dance alone. Leave it on and fourteen copies of the same face follow half a beat behind on the bleachers.

## Why make it bad on purpose

You've probably seen the GENER8ION and Yung Lean video for "STORM" (April 2026, directed by Romain Gavras): a boarding school in Leeds in 2034, a hundred boys in uniform lined up for a class photo, and that choreography. It picked up four VMA nominations this year, and then the "me doing STORM" videos took over. Most were made with video-to-video motion transfer tools like Higgsfield, and the director [complained publicly](https://stereogum.com/2515160/romain-gavras-addresses-slop-copies-of-gener8ion-yung-lean-storm-video/news) about the AI copies.

I wanted one too, but this blog has three rules: client-side only, zero server cost, no external APIs. A video diffusion model breaks all three. So I went the other way. Take only the joint coordinates from the video, cut the face out of a photo as an oval, and build the body in three.js from rods and boxes like a paper doll. Give up on looking real from the start and you get something that runs in the browser immediately. And the badness is pretty funny.

## How it's built

### 1. Pulling the joints out of the video

I used MediaPipe's Pose Landmarker. Give it one image and it returns the positions of 33 joints: nose, eyes, ears, shoulders, elbows, wrists, hips, knees, ankles and so on. It gives them two ways: as 2D positions on the image, and as **3D coordinates in metres** with the origin between the hips. The 3D set is what makes a turn come out as a turn. Each joint also carries a visibility score; legs cut off by the frame still get estimated coordinates, but with low visibility.

I saved the music video locally and ran 37 seconds from 4:18 through Python at 15 frames per second. The problem was a hundred people in every frame. My first pass took "the biggest person nearest the centre", which hopped to a neighbour every few frames. Preferring whoever was closest to the previous pick helped until it locked onto the wrong person and followed him instead.

In the end I found him by his shirt. In this scene Yung Lean is the only one without a blazer, in a white shirt among a hundred navy jackets. Convert the frame to HSV, take the largest bright, unsaturated blob, and that's the shirt. Crop a body-sized window around it, scale it up, run the pose model on that, and keep only a pose whose torso sits on the blob. Even in wide shots where he's five percent of the frame, this holds.

One stretch still got away. After the smoking close-up he turns and walks into the crowd for nine seconds showing only his back, and the model kept grabbing the faces of the boys around him. I cut it. What's left is three seconds of close-up and 24 seconds of standing still, the crowd starting to heave, and the group choreography.

Cleanup goes like this. If the shoulders jump more than eight percent of the frame in one step, that's someone else, so the frame is dropped. Gaps are interpolated from their neighbours and a three-frame average takes the jitter out. One more thing: MediaPipe's 3D coordinates are relative to the camera, so a camera looking down tilts everyone backwards. Since most frames are standing, I took the median of the hips-to-shoulders direction and rotated the whole clip until it pointed straight up. It came to about 11 degrees. Keep 17 of the 33 joints, store millimetres as integers, and 27 seconds comes to 113KB of JSON. Those numbers are the only thing on the page. Not a single frame of video is included.

### 2. Rods between the coordinates

With 3D coordinates there's hardly any rigging. Put a sphere at each joint in a three.js scene, a cylinder between each pair, and every frame stretch and turn the cylinder to fit. A unit cylinder is made once; its position is the midpoint, its length the distance, and its orientation the quaternion that turns the up vector toward the second point.

```ts
function fitCylinder(m: THREE.Mesh, a: THREE.Vector3, b: THREE.Vector3, radius: number) {
	const dir = b.clone().sub(a);
	m.position.copy(a).addScaledVector(dir, 0.5);
	m.quaternion.setFromUnitVectors(UP, dir.clone().normalize());
	m.scale.set(radius, dir.length(), radius);
}
```

The torso is one box. Take hip centre to shoulder centre as up, the shoulder line as right, their cross product as forward, and you have a frame to fit a shoulder-width by torso-length box into. The tie is a thin bar on its front. The head is a sphere between the ears, and the direction from ear centre to nose is where the face points, so the face oval is glued ten centimetres out along it. Turn around and the face disappears and you see the back of the head, which is the reward for doing this in 3D.

The data is 15 frames per second and the screen draws 60, so the position is interpolated between neighbouring frames. To keep the feet off thin air, the whole doll is raised or lowered each frame until the lower ankle touches the floor.

three.js comes from npm and goes into the bundle. It isn't fetched from someone else's server; it lives in this blog's static files, so it will still run in a few years. The whole library would be big, but only what's used survives bundling, and gzipped that is 187KB.

### 3. The face is cut out as an oval

The original plan was to run MediaPipe's face segmentation in the browser and trace the hairline automatically. But MediaPipe for the browser needs a wasm runtime and a model file from a CDN, several megabytes together. I could vendor them into the repository, but a glued-on paper doll didn't seem to need that.

So a human does the cutting. Choose a photo and a canvas with an oval appears. Drag to fit your face, scroll or pinch to resize. On confirm, the oval region is copied to a new canvas and multiplied, using the `destination-in` composite mode, by a radial gradient that fades at the edge. That canvas becomes a three.js texture on an oval plane, with a sheet of white paper behind it.

The photo comes in through `<input type="file">` and is used only as an `Image` object. There is no path to a server.

### 4. Classmates

The fun of the original is a hundred people doing the same thing. So two rows of bleachers go behind, with fourteen more dolls on them. They play the same motion data a few frames late, a little smaller, in navy blazers. They all have the same face. This is the worst part and the funniest.

## Why the real version can't run in a browser

The "me doing STORM" videos are video-to-video motion transfer. The motion of the original is the condition, and the model generates new pixels of the person in the photo performing it. That's a video diffusion model: billions of parameters, tens of gigabytes of memory, and somewhere between tens of seconds and minutes on a server GPU for a clip a few seconds long. Even with WebGPU, the weights won't fit in a page, and even if they did you'd be staring at a progress bar instead of touching anything. Send it to a server and every run costs money, and at that point it's a service, not a useless toy.

Pose estimation sits at the opposite end. The model is a few megabytes and its whole job is answering "where are the joints" with numbers, so it runs in real time on a phone. The doll in this post is that difference made visible. Understanding motion is cheap and rendering it is expensive. Give up on rendering and use a paper doll, and the rest is free.

## Wrapping up

My favourite thing about the result is switching on "Show skeleton". The stick figure beside the doll and the doll itself are visibly looking at the same numbers. Those numbers are the only thing taken from the video. Everything else is drawn. The legs flail now and then because the source is mostly upper-body shots and the model guessed them, and I left that alone. Bad is the concept.

The doll's code is in the [repository](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/src/components/storm), and the Python scripts that find the white shirt, extract the joints and clean them are in [scripts/storm-pose](https://github.com/GUMBOKIM/gumbo-blog/tree/main/scripts/storm-pose).
